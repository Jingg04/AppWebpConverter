Print vs Digital Poster Design: 300 DPI, CMYK, Bleeds vs 72 DPI sRGB WebP

M
Marcus Vance Principal Image Processing Engineer
Published: 2026-11-11 8 min read ✓ Verified Technical Review
Table of Contents

Designing a poster for a 4K OLED screen is a completely different engineering challenge than preparing a poster for an offset commercial printing press. If you send a 72 DPI web graphic to a print shop, your poster will arrive pixelated and blurry; if you ignore print bleeds, mechanical paper cutters will leave ugly white hairline borders. Master the technical differences between digital display and physical print.

The Technical Specification Matrix

Parameter Digital Display (Web & Social) Commercial Physical Print
Target Resolution 72 to 144 PPI: Screen pixel density 300 DPI: Continuous halftone dot density
Color Space sRGB / Display P3: Additive light CMYK (FOGRA39 / US Web Coated): Subtractive ink
Margins & Bleeds 0px bleed (exact bounding box) 3mm (0.125 in) Bleed: Mandatory for cutting
Optimal File Format WebP / SVG: Lightweight & fast PDF/X-1a / TIFF: Vector fonts embedded

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do bright neon colors look dull in print?

Computer screens emit additive RGB light capable of displaying ultra-vibrant neon greens and electric blues. Physical printers use subtractive CMYK ink absorption (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black), which has a smaller color gamut and cannot physically reproduce neon wavelengths.

What is a bleed margin and why is 3mm mandatory?

Paper cutters slice through thousands of printed sheets at high speed. A 3mm (0.125 inch) bleed extends your background artwork past the cut line, preventing accidental white hairline borders if the blade shifts slightly.

What pixel resolution is required for a standard 24×36 inch poster at 300 DPI?

24 inches × 300 DPI = 7200 pixels width; 36 inches × 300 DPI = 10800 pixels height. That equals a massive 77.7 megapixel file.
